# The OSCE Under Critique: Classic and Recent

*A focused deep-dive for the RANZCP redesign — because the College has already removed the
OSCE, the live question is no longer "should we?" but "was the reasoning sound, and is the
replacement defensible?" This document marshals the evidence on both sides.*

> Full verified citations for everything below are in
> [`annotated-bibliography.md`](./annotated-bibliography.md) (Sections C and F).

---

## 1. The classic critique (Hodges, 1999–2013)

Brian Hodges built the intellectual case against over-reliance on the OSCE:

- **Checklists don't capture expertise (1999).** On standardised-patient interviews, global
  ratings rose with expertise but *checklist scores fell* — experts ask fewer, more targeted
  questions while checklists reward exhaustive box-ticking. *(This particular study used
  family-practice SPs, not a psychiatric station — Hodges' psychiatry-OSCE evidence is in his
  separate 1998/2002 Toronto papers.)*
- **The OSCE constructs competence as "performance" (2003; socio-history, 2009).** Its
  celebrated objectivity was partly bought by fragmenting performance into discrete,
  time-limited stations — which quietly redefines what competence *is*, privileging the
  "competent performer" over integrated, relational practice.
- **"Learning to love the subjective and collective" (2013).** The constructive turn:
  rehabilitate expert judgement and narrative rather than chase psychometric objectivity. This
  is the intellectual warrant for programmatic, portfolio-based assessment — i.e., for what
  RANZCP is now doing.

**The psychiatry-specific stakes:** the OSCE is least able to assess exactly what psychiatry
cares most about — formulation, therapeutic alliance, use of self — which cannot be performed
against a checklist in a few minutes.

## 2. What the last decade added (2015–2026)

The newer literature does not merely repeat Hodges; it supplies *empirical* force along four
lines that a committee will find harder to wave away.

### A. Authenticity is conditional, not intrinsic
**Yeates et al. (2025, realist evaluation)** show the OSCE only feels authentic when specific
design features are present (whole cases, adequate time, candidate autonomy, minimal examiner
interruption). Conventional fragmented/checklist OSCEs lack ecological validity — students
cannot "project into their future role." **Khan's AMEE Guide No. 81 (2013)** concedes the
format's tendency to *compartmentalise* skills.

### B. "Objectivity" is overstated — examiner cognition is irreducibly subjective
This is the most damaging recent strand:
- **Hyde, Boursicot et al. (2022)** — examiners construct idiosyncratic personal theories of
  performance; some scoring variance arises from raters *compensating for the artificiality of
  the OSCE itself*.
- **Yeates et al. (2015, 2022)** — scores are biased by *contrast effects* (the standard set by
  ~7 preceding candidates) and *drift* over time, and these effects can **flip pass/fail
  decisions**.
- **Gingerich, Kogan et al. (2011, 2014)** — reframe rater "error" as legitimate, systematic
  *social judgement*. True objectivity in the OSCE is unattainable.

The irony for the redesign: this work *undermines the OSCE's historical selling point*
(objectivity) while simultaneously *legitimising* the expert-judgement basis of programmatic
assessment (Hodges 2013). The same evidence that weakens the OSCE strengthens the CCPR.

### C. Equity and differential attainment
A serious, politically salient critique:
- **Woolf et al. (2011)** — consistent underperformance of non-white candidates across UK
  medical assessments (d≈0.42).
- **Esmail & Roberts (2013)** — the GMC-commissioned analysis behind the BAPIO judicial review:
  subjective examiner bias in the face-to-face MRCGP Clinical Skills Assessment may contribute
  to higher failure rates among BME UK graduates and IMGs.
- **Patterson et al. (2018)** — differential attainment is multifactorial ("dark variance"),
  but exam design and acculturation interact to disadvantage IMGs.

This matters acutely for RANZCP given the **AMC's cultural-safety conditions** and a trainee
body with many international medical graduates: a high-stakes, examiner-subjective station exam
is a differential-attainment risk that portfolio assessment can (if designed well) mitigate.

### D. The COVID reappraisal and virtual OSCEs
- **"Has the OSCE Met Its Final Demise?" (Front Med, 2022)** — uses pandemic disruption to argue
  for permanently rebalancing away from sole reliance on the in-person OSCE.
- **Reay, Maguire & Looi (2024, Australas Psychiatry)** — RANZCP-relevant evaluation of the
  *virtual* psychiatry OSCE's equity and fairness.

### E. The technical critique: standard-setting in small cohorts
**Homer et al. (2020)** show that *borderline regression* — the near-universal OSCE pass/fail
method — becomes unreliable in small cohorts. Specialty exams with modest candidate numbers (a
psychiatry college's situation) are exactly where this bites.

## 3. The counter-argument — keep it honest

Removing the OSCE is **contested**, and the committee should not present it as settled:
- **Amos, Weightman & Miller (2023)** modelled trainees transitioning to Fellowship under the
  WBA-based Alternative Assessment Pathway and argued it may be *less* effective than the OSCE
  at detecting pre-competent trainees.
- **Amos et al. (2024)** critically examine the merit/equity/logistics rationale for retirement.
- General medical press (AusDoc, InSight+/MJA) has framed the removal as risky.

The legitimate worry: a portfolio synthesised from in-training assessments and supervisor
observations can be *gamed or inflated* if it lacks enough genuinely independent, observed,
standardised sampling of performance. "Learning to love the subjective" must not slide into
"tolerating the unstandardised."

---

## 4. Implications for the RANZCP redesign

1. **The decision to retire the OSCE is well-supported** by the authenticity, examiner-cognition,
   equity, and standard-setting literatures — and is reinforced by, not just consistent with,
   Hodges.
2. **But the replacement must answer Amos et al. directly.** The CCPR should guarantee enough
   *independent, observed* performance sampling (IOCA and beyond) to defend entrustment
   decisions, with explicit standards, triangulation, and defensible aggregation rules.
3. **Build in the equity safeguards** the OSCE lacked: examiner calibration, bias-aware
   assessor training, monitoring of differential attainment by IMG status / ethnicity /
   first-language, aligned to the AMC cultural-safety conditions.
4. **Keep observed sampling of relational competence.** The point of leaving the OSCE was that a
   station can't assess alliance and use of self — so the portfolio must assess them
   *somewhere*, through longitudinal observed encounters, not infer them away.
5. **Treat examiner subjectivity as signal, not noise** (Gingerich; Hodges 2013) — design for
   well-trained assessors making defensible judgements, rather than pretending a rubric removes
   judgement.

**Bottom line:** the modern evidence has moved decisively from "is the OSCE objective and fair?"
(answer: less than claimed) to "how do we build a *trustworthy programme* of assessment?"
(Schuwirth & van der Vleuten, 2024). RANZCP is on the right side of that shift — provided the
CCPR is engineered to be as rigorous as it is humane.
