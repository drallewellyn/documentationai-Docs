# The Psychiatric Core: Identity, Supervision, Feedback & Evaluation

*The third strand of the evidence pack. The briefing paper covers the CBME architecture, the
OSCE critique, and learning theory. This document covers what is distinctively psychiatric —
the formation of the clinician, the supervisory relationship, feedback, and how to evaluate the
program itself.*

> Full verified citations are in [`annotated-bibliography.md`](./annotated-bibliography.md)
> (Section G).

---

## 1. Professional identity formation — psychiatry's real curriculum

In psychiatry the clinician's *self* is the instrument. Identity formation is therefore not a
soft adjunct to training; it is the core, and it should be designed and assessed deliberately.

- **Cruess & Cruess et al. (2014, 2015)** reframed medical education around *professional
  identity formation* (PIF) rather than just teaching professionalism as a set of behaviours —
  becoming a psychiatrist, not merely doing psychiatry.
- **Cruess, Cruess & Steinert (2016), "Amending Miller's pyramid"** adds an **"is"** apex
  (identity) above Miller's **"does."** This is directly actionable for RANZCP assessment design:
  it legitimises assessing *who the trainee is becoming* (use of self, values, reflective
  capacity), not only what they can do in an observed encounter.
- **Foundational lineage:** Cruess & Cruess, "Professionalism must be taught" (*BMJ*, 1997).

**Design implication:** the CCPR portfolio is an ideal vehicle for evidencing identity formation
over time — reflective writing, supervisor narratives, the psychotherapy written case — provided
these are valued as data, not treated as box-ticking.

## 2. Reflective practice — the engine of identity and psychotherapy training

- **Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983)** gave us reflection-in-action and
  reflection-on-action, and the image of the "swampy lowlands" of real practice versus the "high
  ground" of technical rationality — apt for psychiatry, which lives in the swamp.
- **Schön, *Educating the Reflective Practitioner* (1987)** — the follow-up, and the more useful
  text for curriculum design: it turns reflection into a *theory of professional education* (the
  "reflective practicum," learning through coaching and supervised practice). This maps almost
  perfectly onto psychiatry's apprenticeship-and-supervision model.
- **Kolb (1984/2015)** — the experiential learning cycle underpins supervised clinical practice.
- **Mann, Gordon & MacLeod (2009)** — systematic review confirming reflection can be taught and
  matters, while cautioning the evidence base is uneven (so design reflection purposefully, not
  ritualistically).

## 3. Supervision and Balint — psychiatry's signature pedagogy

Psychiatry treats supervision (including of psychotherapy, transference and countertransference)
as a *core competency*, not mere oversight.

- **Kilminster et al., AMEE Guide No. 27 (2007)** — the standard reference on what makes clinical
  supervision effective: the supervisory relationship is the single most important variable.
- **Graham, Gask et al. (2009, *Academic Psychiatry*)** — psychiatry-specific evaluation of
  Balint-style case-discussion groups for processing the doctor–patient relationship. *(A
  commonly-miscited paper — it is in Academic Psychiatry, not Psychiatric Bulletin.)*
- **Dreyfus (2004) five-stage skill-acquisition model** — the novice→expert progression that
  underpins both Milestones and entrustment, and gives supervisors a shared language for "where
  is this trainee?"

**Design implication:** invest in *supervisor* capability (see §5) and make Balint/reflective
supervision a structured, protected component — not an optional extra.

## 4. Feedback — the mechanism that makes WBA work

Workplace-based assessment and entrustment are only as good as the feedback culture around them.

- **Ende (1983)** — the classic statement of principles.
- **Hattie & Timperley (2007)** — the dominant evidence-based model (feed-up / feed-back /
  feed-forward); broadly applicable.
- **van de Ridder et al. (2008)** — clarifies what "feedback" actually is in clinical education.
- **Sargeant et al. (2015), the R2C2 model** — a relationship-centred, evidence-based *coaching*
  model (build Relationship, explore Reactions and Content, Coach for change). It is
  particularly well-matched to the reflective, alliance-based nature of psychiatric supervision,
  and is the natural feedback model to pair with the CCPR.

**Design implication:** frame WBA data as *coaching for growth*, not surveillance — directly
mitigating the assessment-burden/surveillance risk flagged in the main briefing (Schut, Roberts,
Szulewski).

## 5. Faculty development — the highest-leverage, most-neglected investment

Everything above — supervision, feedback, entrustment, identity coaching — depends on the
supervisor's capability. *(Steinert et al., BEME Guides 8 and 40 — cited in the briefing.)* If
the redesign under-invests anywhere at its peril, it is here: a programmatic, judgement-based
system asks far more of supervisors than a checklist OSCE did.

## 6. Program evaluation — how you will know the redesign worked

Design the evaluation in from the start (it is Step 6 of Kern):

- **Kirkpatrick's four levels** (reaction → learning → behaviour → results), with the
  **Barr/Hammick/Freeth** medical-education adaptation (2a/2b learning, 4a/4b results) — the
  standard outcomes framework.
- **Stufflebeam's CIPP model** (Context, Input, Process, Product) — a decision-oriented
  alternative better suited to *continuous program improvement* than to one-off judgement.
- **Cook & Beckman (2006)** — the standard reference on validity and reliability if you intend to
  evaluate (or publish on) the program's own assessment instruments — which RANZCP should, to
  answer critics like Amos et al. with its own evidence.

**Design implication:** pre-commit to an evaluation plan and a small set of outcome measures
(including differential-attainment monitoring and entrustment-decision defensibility) so the
redesign generates the evidence that the OSCE-removal debate currently lacks.
