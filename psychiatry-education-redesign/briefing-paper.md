# Designing the Next RANZCP Psychiatry Training Program: An Evidence Briefing

*A briefing for the curriculum redesign — written to be defensible in front of a
committee that includes both competency-based medical education (CBME) enthusiasts and
sceptics.*

## 0. Where the RANZCP program actually stands (the live context)

Any "redesign" briefing has to start from where the program *is*, because several of the
debates below are no longer hypothetical — the College has already moved.

- **The program is competency-based.** The **Competency-Based Fellowship Program (CBFP)**
  (a.k.a. the 2012 Fellowship Program) runs over 60 months across three stages —
  **Basic (Stage 1) → Proficient (Stage 2) → Advanced (Stage 3)** — and already uses
  **Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs)** (roughly 25–30 entrusted for Fellowship)
  supported by five formative workplace-based assessment (WBA) tools: **Case-based
  Discussion, mini-CEX, Professional Presentation, DOPS, and the Observed Clinical Activity
  (OCA)** — now joined by the **Independent OCA (IOCA)**.
- **The OSCE has been removed.** Suspended during COVID-19, replaced by the Clinical
  Competency Assessment (CCA) and its Modified Portfolio Review, and from **September 2026**
  superseded by the **Clinical Competency Portfolio Review (CCPR)** — a **programmatic,
  portfolio-based** judgement synthesising Stage 3 in-training assessments, OCAs and IOCAs
  rather than a single high-stakes station exam. The removal is **contested** (see §2).
- **A full curriculum redesign is underway.** Led with **ACER** (Australian Council for
  Educational Research) across 2023–2024 and driven by **AMC accreditation conditions**
  (notably around curriculum structure, assessment, and cultural safety), it is realigning
  graduate outcomes to **CanMEDS 2015**, with the new syllabus targeted for implementation
  around **2028**.

**Why this matters for the briefing:** the College has, in effect, already *acted on* the
two critiques the committee asked about — it has moved away from the OSCE (Hodges' target)
and toward programmatic assessment. The intellectual task now is to do that *well*, avoiding
the documented failure modes of CBME and programmatic assessment, rather than to relitigate
whether to adopt them.

## 1. The architecture: CBME, EPAs and what they buy you

The modern scaffolding — and why each piece is in the program:

- **CanMEDS 2015** (Frank, Snell, Sherbino) gives the role-based outcomes vocabulary
  (Medical Expert plus the six "intrinsic" roles). It is the explicit alignment target of
  the RANZCP redesign, and it suits psychiatry, which leans heavily on the intrinsic roles
  (Communicator, Collaborator, Health Advocate, Professional).
- **EPAs** (ten Cate) translate abstract competencies into units of professional work a
  supervisor can *entrust*. They are the practical bridge between a competency framework and
  day-to-day supervision, and they are already central to the CBFP.
- **Programmatic assessment** (van der Vleuten & Schuwirth) is the principle that no single
  assessment is adequate; competence is inferred from many low- and high-stakes data points
  combined by expert judgement. The CCPR *is* programmatic assessment in action.
- **Mastery learning** (McGaghie) and **deliberate practice** (Ericsson) supply the learning
  science underneath CBME's promise: fixed outcomes, variable time, repeated feedback-rich
  practice to a defined standard.

**The honest framing for the committee:** CBME's promise is real and the College has
committed to it. But "theory to practice" (Frank et al., 2010) warned, and a decade of
evidence has confirmed, that the *implementation* is where programs succeed or fail. The
rest of this briefing is about the failure modes — and how the design avoids them.

## 2. The OSCE critique — and why RANZCP's portfolio turn is defensible

Brian Hodges' body of work is the intellectual backbone for moving away from the OSCE, and
it is worth getting the evidence exactly right.

- **Hodges, Regehr, McNaughton, Tiberius & Hanson (1999), "OSCE checklists do not capture
  increasing levels of expertise."** The pivotal empirical finding: on standardised-patient
  interviews, **global ratings rose with expertise but checklist scores did not — and in
  fact *fell*** as experts asked fewer, more targeted questions while checklists rewarded
  exhaustive box-ticking. *Correction to an earlier verbal account: this particular study
  used **family-practice** standardised patients, not a psychiatric station.* The
  psychiatry-specific evidence lives in Hodges' separate Toronto psychiatry-OSCE work
  (validation, 1998; the *Academic Psychiatry* faculty guide, 2002; "the challenge of
  creating new OSCE measures to capture expertise," 2002) — all cited in the bibliography.
- **Hodges, "Validity and the OSCE" (2003)** and **"OSCE! Variations on a theme by Harden"
  (2003)** argue the OSCE's celebrated objectivity was partly bought by *fragmenting*
  performance into discrete, standardised stations — which quietly changes what "competence"
  is taken to mean.
- **Hodges, *The Objective Structured Clinical Examination: A Socio-History* (2009)** is the
  full discourse analysis: the OSCE doesn't merely *measure* competence, it *produces* a
  particular conception of it — the "competent performer" demonstrating discrete behaviours
  in a time-limited cubicle. For psychiatry, that risks crowding out the integrated,
  relational, longitudinal competence (formulation, therapeutic alliance, use of self) that
  cannot be performed against a checklist in five minutes.
- **Hodges, "Assessment in the post-psychometric era: learning to love the subjective and
  collective" (2013)** is the constructive sequel — and the single most useful Hodges piece
  for the redesign. It argues for *rehabilitating expert judgement and narrative* rather than
  chasing psychometric objectivity. This is the explicit intellectual warrant for the CCPR.

**The recent evidence (2015–2026) reinforces Hodges** along four lines — authenticity is
conditional not intrinsic (Yeates 2025); examiner "objectivity" is a myth (Hyde/Boursicot 2022;
Yeates' contrast/sequence effects 2015/2022; Gingerich's rater-as-social-judge work); the OSCE
carries documented *equity/differential-attainment* risk (Woolf 2011; Esmail & Roberts 2013);
and borderline-regression standard-setting is unreliable in the small cohorts typical of a
specialty college (Homer 2020). The full treatment is in
[`osce-critiques.md`](./osce-critiques.md). The striking point for the committee: the same
evidence that *weakens* the OSCE's objectivity claim *legitimises* the expert-judgement basis of
the CCPR.

**But hold the tension honestly.** Removing the OSCE is contested. **Amos, Weightman & Miller
(2023, *Australasian Psychiatry*)** modelled trainees transitioning to Fellowship under the
OSCE-replacement pathway and raised concerns about trainees progressing before demonstrating
competence; their 2024 companion commentary critiques the merit/equity/logistics rationale, and
commentary in the general medical press has called the removal risky. The committee should treat
the portfolio turn not as "the OSCE was bad" but as **"a single station exam is the wrong tool
for relational competence, *provided* the programmatic replacement is rigorous, defensible, and
genuinely samples performance."** That proviso is the work.

## 3. The CBME critique — a map of the failure modes (not an argument to retreat)

These critiques are not reasons to abandon CBME; they are a precise map of how it goes wrong.
The committee should read them as a pre-mortem.

| Failure mode | Source | What it should change in the design |
|---|---|---|
| **Reductionism** — fragmenting holistic practice into atomised, checkable units | Talbot, "Monkey see, monkey do" (2004); Hodges, "tea-steeping or i-Doc" (2010) | Keep integrative, holistic judgement points (the CCPR); resist ever-finer checklists |
| **Competencies aren't independently measurable** | Lurie, Mooney & Lyness (2009) | Treat competencies/EPAs as a *shared language* and synthesise via expert judgement — not as independent variables to be metered |
| **Milestones bureaucracy** outweighing educational yield | Norman, Norcini & Bordage, "milestones or millstones?" (2014) | Keep documentation proportionate to educational value |
| **EPA over-enthusiasm** | Krupat, "Critical thoughts about the Core EPAs" (2018) | Define EPAs sparingly and carefully; validate entrustment decisions |
| **Assessment burden / surveillance** — trainees experiencing programmatic assessment as relentless monitoring | Schut et al. (2020); Roberts et al. (2022); Szulewski et al. (2023) | Design WBA/CCPR *volume* for educational value, protect the supervisory relationship, frame data as growth not surveillance |
| **Implementation fidelity** — most "CBME failed" stories are really implementation failures | van Melle et al., Core Components Framework (2019); Touchie & ten Cate (2016) | Use a fidelity framework to evaluate the rollout; expect and manage the perils |

Hodges' **"tea-steeping or i-Doc"** metaphor is worth putting in front of the committee
verbatim: time-based "tea-steeping" (immersion produces a rounded clinician) versus CBME's
"i-Doc" (assemble competencies like modular components). *Each model gains something and
loses something.* The redesign should consciously preserve what tea-steeping protects — the
emergent, hard-to-name qualities of a good psychiatrist — inside a competency frame.

## 4. The learning-theory foundation — and why andragogy should *not* be load-bearing

A deliberate position for the committee, and a defensible one:

**Adult learning theory (andragogy) is a teaching philosophy, not a validated evidence base,
and it should not be used to justify under-structuring the program.**

- **Norman, "The adult learner: a mythical species" (1999)** is the citable basis: the
  supposed special characteristics of adult learners (self-direction, problem-centredness)
  are not empirically distinct from how anyone learns. Knowles' andragogy/pedagogy dichotomy
  is false — a PGY-2 starting psychiatry is a *novice* who benefits from structure and
  direction, not from being told to self-direct.
- **Why this protects the redesign:** CBME programs that lean on andragogy tend to use
  "self-directed learning" to justify thin scaffolding ("the trainee will identify their own
  needs"). That is exactly the under-structuring the CBME critics warn about. Healthy
  scepticism about andragogy *closes* that gap.

**Build instead on frameworks with real empirical traction:**

- **Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan; applied to medicine in ten Cate, Kusurkar &
  Williams, *AMEE Guide No. 59*, 2011) — the robust replacement for the motivational half of
  andragogy. It explains *when* autonomy helps (once competence and relatedness are in
  place), rather than assuming adults always want it.
- **Cognitive Load Theory** (Sweller) and **van Merriënboer's 4C/ID** ("Ten Steps to Complex
  Learning") — instructional-design science purpose-built for complex professional skills
  like assessment and formulation.
- **The cognitive science of learning** — retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving
  (Dunlosky et al., 2013) — high-yield, replicated, and chronically under-used in
  postgraduate curricula.
- **Constructive alignment** (Biggs & Tang) — outcomes, teaching/learning activities and
  assessment must align; this is the operational backbone of good course design and dovetails
  with Kern's six-step approach.
- **Active learning** (Freeman et al., 2014) and **simulation with deliberate practice**
  (McGaghie et al.) — justify designing contact time around application, and using simulated
  patients for risk assessment, MSE, de-escalation and breaking bad news.
- **Situated learning / communities of practice** (Lave & Wenger) — the genuinely robust
  account of how professional identity and tacit knowledge form in clinical teams; central to
  psychiatry.
- **Faculty development** (Steinert et al., BEME Guides 8 and 40) — a program is only as good
  as its supervisors; the evidence says deliberate faculty development is what makes WBA and
  feedback actually work. **This is the highest-leverage, most-neglected investment** in any
  CBME rollout.

## 5. Recommendations for the committee

1. **Stay the course on CBME and programmatic assessment, but design against the failure
   modes.** Adopt van Melle's Core Components Framework as the *fidelity* yardstick for the
   rollout, and Touchie & ten Cate's "perils" list as a standing risk register.
2. **Make the CCPR rigorous, not just narrative.** Hodges (2013) legitimises expert judgement
   — but answer Amos et al. (2023) directly: ensure the portfolio *samples enough observed
   performance* (OCA/IOCA) to defend entrustment decisions, with clear standards and
   triangulation. "Learning to love the subjective" must not become "tolerating the
   unstandardised."
3. **Protect the relationship.** Treat assessment-burden/surveillance (Schut, Roberts,
   Szulewski) as a first-order design constraint: fewer, better, developmental data points;
   coaching framing; protected supervisory time.
4. **Anchor in learning science, not andragogy.** Build explicitly on constructive alignment,
   cognitive load / 4C/ID, retrieval-and-spacing, deliberate practice, and Self-Determination
   Theory. Use andragogy's respect for trainees' experience, but do not let it license
   under-structuring.
5. **Invest disproportionately in faculty development.** It is the dependency on which WBA,
   feedback, and entrustment all rest.
6. **Keep the psychiatry-specific core visible inside the competency frame.** Formulation,
   therapeutic alliance, supervision of psychotherapy, use of self, and professional identity
   formation are what tea-steeping protected; design assessment that makes them *count*.

## Appendix: the through-line in one paragraph

General medical education supplies the *machinery* — CanMEDS, EPAs, programmatic assessment,
Kern, mastery learning. The critics (Hodges on the OSCE; Talbot, Lurie, Whitehead and the
assessment-burden literature on CBME) supply the *map of failure modes* — reductionism,
unmeasurable competencies, bureaucratic burden, surveillance. Learning science (constructive
alignment, cognitive load, deliberate practice, retrieval practice, SDT) supplies the *engine*
— and is a far stronger foundation than andragogy, whose "special adult learner" is, on the
evidence, largely mythical. RANZCP has already made the strategic moves (CanMEDS alignment,
EPAs, OSCE removal, portfolio assessment); the redesign's job is to implement them with
fidelity and to keep the distinctively psychiatric, relational core of the discipline
visible and assessed.
